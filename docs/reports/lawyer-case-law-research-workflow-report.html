How lawyers actually search for case law

The search box is only one part of a defensible workflow. Lawyers define the question, identify controlling authority, search by several methods, read the full opinions, update every important case, synthesize the results, and document when the research is complete.

This is a product-design and research-method report, not legal advice. Workflows vary by jurisdiction, organization, matter, and assignment.

Executive answer

Lawyers do not just search for cases; they build and validate a chain of authority. A result is useful only after the researcher knows why it matters, whether the deciding court can bind the relevant court, what proposition the opinion actually holds, and whether later law has weakened or displaced it.
§

Professional guidance consistently describes legal research as a planned, iterative process. Researchers establish scope and jurisdiction, use secondary sources to learn an unfamiliar field, locate primary authority through multiple search methods, validate it, preserve a research trail, and stop only when the results are sufficiently complete for the assignment. [1] [2] [3]

1Search begins before the database.
The assignment, legally significant facts, governing jurisdiction, court level, time period, and deliverable determine what should be searched.
2Relevant is not the same as controlling.
An opinion can match the facts yet remain unusable because it is nonprecedential, outside the jurisdiction, superseded, or procedurally inapposite.
3Discovery is not validation.
Search rankings and citation counts find candidates. They do not establish that a proposition remains good law.
4Completion is coverage, not page count.
Research is defensible when controlling sources and material counterauthority are covered, important cases are updated, methods converge, and remaining gaps are disclosed.
Bottom line for this project Running Google Scholar and CourtListener together is a strong discovery design. Google Scholar should remain the preferred display order, while CourtListener contributes structured metadata, citation lookup, and independent recall. But the extension needs a research-session layer before it can support the larger lawyer workflow.

The end-to-end research loop

This ten-stage model synthesizes the practical guidance from law libraries, court materials, professional competency standards, and the two current source systems. It is not perfectly linear: a good case changes the vocabulary, reveals new issues, and sends the researcher back through the loop.

1

Frame the assignment

Record the question, client or audience, deliverable, deadline, cost constraints, and the factual and procedural context.

Output: research brief
2

Fix the governing frame

Identify jurisdiction, forum, court hierarchy, relevant dates, publication rules, and the sources of law that can control.

Output: authority map
3

Build the issue tree

Break claims, defenses, elements, standards, remedies, and procedural questions into searchable subissues.

Output: issue checklist
4

Learn the vocabulary

When the field is unfamiliar, consult treatises, practice guides, encyclopedias, and annotations for doctrine, terms, and seed citations.

Output: terms + seed authorities
5

Search in several ways

Combine known-item lookup, natural language, Boolean/field queries, facts and posture, digests or headnotes, and statute-linked cases.

Output: candidate set + query log
6

Follow the citation network

Use one useful opinion to move backward to its authorities, forward to later treatment, and sideways to related cases.

Output: citation trail
7

Triage by authority and fit

Prioritize controlling, precedential, current decisions that address the same issue and procedural posture; preserve contrary authority.

Output: review queue
8

Read and brief the full opinion

Verify facts, posture, issue, rule, holding, reasoning, disposition, separate opinions, quotations, and pin cites.

Output: case note
9

Validate and update

Check direct history and later treatment in a citator, inspect treatment at the relevant point of law, and read material citing opinions.

Output: validation record
10

Synthesize, write, and close

Organize authority by issue, explain both supporting and adverse law, fill gaps revealed by drafting, and record why the search can stop.

Output: memo + research record
New facts, terminology, citing decisions, or drafting gaps → revise the issue tree and search again.

Support: Georgetown recommends defining the assignment, isolating legally significant facts, and making a research plan before searching; AALL's competency standards call for governing authority, validation, documentation, and appropriate stopping; Berkeley describes research as iterative. [2] [3] [4]

How lawyers find cases in practice

No single query style is sufficient. Lawyers switch methods because each method has different failure modes: terminology changes, opinions omit an expected phrase, factual similarity can hide behind doctrinal labels, and a known citation can unlock an entire line of cases.

Start broad enough to learn

Early searches reveal the vocabulary, doctrinal structure, and leading authorities. Cornell and the Library of Congress both distinguish primary authority from explanatory secondary sources and recognize the latter as an efficient starting point in an unfamiliar field. [1] [5]

Then search against your first answer

A defensible search deliberately seeks contrary holdings, limiting language, other jurisdictions, procedural distinctions, and newer authority. This reduces confirmation bias and improves the final explanation of uncertainty.

A useful result page is a triage surface, not the research product. Lawyers scan case identity, court, date, citation, posture, issue language, treatment signals, and snippets to decide what to open. They cannot reliably extract a holding from the result snippet alone.

Authority and relevance are separate questions

A case can be textually similar yet legally weak. Lawyers first ask what can bind the deciding court, then evaluate whether the case actually addresses the same issue under a compatible procedural and factual setting.

1. Governing sourceIs the proposition controlled by a constitution, statute, regulation, rule, or binding appellate decision?
2. Court relationshipDoes the deciding court sit above the relevant court in the same hierarchy, or is the opinion merely persuasive?
3. Precedential statusIs the opinion published or designated precedential, and may it be cited under the jurisdiction's rules?
4. Current validityWas the judgment vacated, reversed, or superseded? Has the relevant proposition been criticized, limited, or displaced?
5. Issue and posture fitDoes the opinion decide the same legal issue under the relevant standard, burden, claim stage, or standard of review?
6. Factual fitWhich facts mattered to the court's reasoning, and how do they compare to the present facts?
7. Time and legal changeIs there newer controlling law, an en banc decision, or an amended statute, rule, or regulation?

The vertical hierarchy is fundamental: federal appellate decisions bind lower federal courts within that circuit, while state high-court decisions bind lower courts in that state on state law; the precise rules become more nuanced across sovereign systems and for predictions of state law. [6] [7]

Do not collapse legal authority into one opaque relevance score. The extension may rank candidates for review, but it should expose the underlying dimensions. Binding status, validity, precedential status, issue fit, and factual fit are different judgments; a single score can hide a fatal defect.

Nonprecedential opinions

Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 32.1 limits restrictions on citing federal unpublished dispositions issued on or after 1 January 2007, but it does not decide their precedential effect. State rules can differ substantially. [8]

Persuasive cases

An out-of-jurisdiction or lower-court opinion may still be useful for reasoning, factual analogy, or an open question. The product should label it persuasive rather than exclude it or present it as controlling.

The full opinion is the unit of legal analysis

Search snippets, summaries, headnotes, and LLM output are navigation aids. The researcher must read the opinion to determine what the court decided and whether a quotation or proposition is accurately supported.

Identity and posture

  • Full case name and citations
  • Court, panel, date, and docket
  • Procedural history and posture
  • Question presented and standard of review

Decision

  • Material facts
  • Rule and actual holding
  • Reasoning and limiting language
  • Judgment and disposition

Use in the new matter

  • Binding or persuasive status
  • Factual similarities and differences
  • Exact quotations with pin cites
  • Concurrences, dissents, and unresolved issues
Headnotes are excellent finding tools, but they are not the opinion. Digests and editorial classifications can lead a researcher to the relevant passage; the proposition must then be checked against the court's own words and context. The Library of Congress describes headnotes and Key Numbers as research tools, while its research material cautions researchers to read and update the case itself. [9] [10]

ABA Formal Opinion 512 says lawyers using generative AI remain responsible for competent review of its output and must also consider confidentiality, supervision, candor, and reasonable fees. That supports a visible draft label and human-verification workflow. [11]

Citation graphs help discovery; citators support validation

These are related but different jobs. A free cited-by list tells the researcher that another document cites a case. A professional citator adds direct history, editorial or algorithmic treatment, treatment at a particular point of law, and tools for investigating the citing references.

Google itself warns that Scholar legal opinions are provided for informational purposes and does not warrant completeness or accuracy. The Library of Congress describes Scholar's How Cited feature as a useful starting overview but not as authoritative as dedicated citators. [12] [13]

How lawyers decide when to stop

Legal research has no universal “last page.” It stops when the work is proportionate to the assignment and the researcher can explain the coverage, the answer, the uncertainty, and the remaining risk.

  • The question, jurisdiction, forum, dates, and deliverable are confirmed.
  • Each material issue and element has been searched.
  • Controlling primary authority has been located and read.
  • Important cases have been updated through an appropriate citator.
  • Contrary authority and plausible alternative rules were sought.
  • Different search methods converge on the same leading authorities.
  • New searches mostly repeat known cases rather than add insight.
  • Quotations, citations, and pinpoints were verified in the opinion.
  • The answer can be explained on both sides with uncertainty disclosed.
  • Unresolved gaps, inaccessible sources, and time/cost limits are recorded.

Yale's research strategy recommends clarifying scope, keeping detailed notes, planning and timeboxing, and considering convergence across research methods. AALL likewise includes documentation, cost-effective source selection, validation, and knowing when research is complete among core competencies. [16] [3]

Where each source fits

The strongest system is layered. Free search services, official court sites, secondary sources, and citators solve different problems; no single source should be asked to do all of them.

The chosen two-lane search strategy is sensible. For ordinary discovery, start Scholar and CourtListener at the same time, merge only after both lanes finish the requested page wave, deduplicate by citation identity, and order merged matches with Scholar first. Preserve provider provenance and independent pagination. For exhaustive cited-by work, CourtListener may be the practical default while Scholar remains optional because Scholar's legal search has coverage and access constraints.

Google documents the jurisdictions and periods covered by Scholar and disclaims completeness and accuracy. CourtListener documents its search and citation-lookup capabilities. The Library of Congress lists both among useful free starting points while warning that free systems often lack an effective citator. [12] [17] [18]

What this means for the current extension plan

The existing plan covers discovery, page navigation, download, exact-case resolution, cited-by expansion, full-text reading, and optional LLM summaries. Those are valuable operational capabilities. The missing layer is the structure that turns downloaded opinions into a defensible research record.

Central safety rule Neither a generated summary nor a cited-by count should display “good law.” The strongest truthful default is: full opinion downloaded; summary generated from this text; external citator not checked.

Recommended design: one extension, one research session

Keep one extension and the focused operational modes already planned. Add a persistent research_session object that every search, read, download, cited-by, summary, and validation action can update. This adds the professional workflow without fragmenting the interface into many unrelated extensions.

Session context

Research question, audience, jurisdiction, forum, relevant dates, facts, posture, deadline, budget, and adverse theory.

Research state

Issue tree, search vocabulary, query log, source coverage, result dispositions, citation trails, and follow-up tasks.

Case state

Canonical identity, authority class, publication status, case note, quotes, validation status, synthesis tags, and rejection reason.

External citator integration should begin as a handoff, not unauthorized automation. The extension can prepare canonical citations, links where available, and a checklist; the lawyer can perform the authenticated KeyCite, Shepard's, or BCite review and record the result. Deeper integration should require an authorized API or licensed workflow.

Practical delivery roadmap

Do not rebuild the already-working browser logic. Add the larger workflow in layers, keeping the current fixture-first and resumable architecture.

Research-session foundation

Add the matter frame, issue tree, query/source log, case status, and immutable provenance. Existing search and download actions may optionally attach to a session.

Authority-aware review queue

Capture jurisdiction, court level, publication/precedential status where reliably available, posture notes, issue tags, and selected/rejected reasons. Expose missing data rather than guessing.

Evidence-linked case notes

Extend the summary toggle into a structured case brief with verified quotations, pinpoints when available, source hashes, and a permanent “not citator-validated” banner by default.

Validation handoff

Create a checklist for direct history and treatment, export canonical citations to a licensed citator workflow, and record provider, date, result, relevant headnote/proposition, and reviewer notes.

Issue synthesis and completeness report

Generate an authority table by issue, controlling/persuasive split, supporting/adverse cases, unreviewed leads, provider coverage, unresolved gaps, and the stopping checklist.

Broaden beyond cases only when needed

A complete legal-research product eventually needs statutes, regulations, rules, constitutional provisions, and secondary sources. Keep that separate from the first case-law workflow milestone.

Acceptance tests for the bigger-picture workflow

  • A downloaded and summarized case never becomes “validated” merely because cited-by collection completed.
  • Every result retains source, query, page/cursor, retrieval time, canonical identity, and deduplication evidence.
  • The same case can support one issue and be adverse on another; status is proposition-aware, not only document-wide.
  • Binding status is either explained from recorded jurisdiction/court data or marked unknown.
  • A summary quotation cannot be emitted as verified unless it matches the downloaded opinion text.
  • The session distinguishes “not searched,” “searched with no result,” “found but unread,” “reviewed,” and “externally validated.”
  • The final export identifies material contrary authority and unresolved coverage limitations.
  • The stopping record reports what was searched, what converged, what was unavailable, and who made the completion judgment.
